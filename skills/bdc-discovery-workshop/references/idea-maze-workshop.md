# Idea Maze Workshop

Use this reference when the founder wants to map a startup idea, understand the strategic decision tree, or explain why their path works now.

## Facilitation Rules

- Ask one question per turn.
- Keep feedback to 2-3 sentences before the next question.
- Reject pitch language. Ask for branch choices, evidence, and named examples.
- Treat missing competitor or casualty knowledge as a research gap, not as a minor detail.
- Separate the path from the timing: what choice is being made, and why that choice became viable now.
- Do not accept "we will execute better" as the reason the path works.

## Exercise Flow

1. Establish the entrance to the maze as a one-sentence category-level idea.
2. Map the major decision branches and options in the category.
3. Identify failed paths and specific companies that hit those dead ends.
4. Identify winners, the exact paths they took, and why those paths worked at that time.
5. Force the founder to state their exact branch-by-branch path.
6. Pressure-test why this path leads to treasure now.
7. Name what would make the chosen path a dead end and what signals to watch.

Ask these questions in order, one at a time:

1. What's the one-sentence version of the idea, the entrance to the maze?
2. What are the major decision branches anyone entering this maze has to make? List the categories and the options within each.
3. For each branch, which paths have been tried and failed, and why did they fail? Name specific companies.
4. Which paths produced winners, and what specifically made that path work at that time?
5. Which exact path are you taking through the maze? Write it as a sequence of branch choices, not a pitch.
6. Why does this specific path lead to treasure today rather than to a dead end?
7. What would have to be true for this path to turn out to be a dead end? What signals should you watch?

## Output Format

When complete, produce:

```markdown
## Idea Maze Summary

Entrance:

Major branches:

Chosen path:

Why now:

Past casualties:

Relevant winners:

Dead-end risks:

Research gaps:
```

Keep each field specific enough that another founder, investor, or advisor could challenge it.
